About

Six years moving between the outcrop, the rig floor, the seismic workstation and the notebook.

I started in geology because of rocks you can hold. I stayed because of the question underneath them: what is down there, and how sure can we be? Every step since has been an attempt to answer that with better evidence.

Field work in Pakistan taught me where geological data actually comes from — cuttings under a hand lens, a log run at three in the morning, a seismic line that does not quite tie. Interpretation work taught me how those pieces are assembled into a subsurface model, and where that model is weakest.

My graduate research at China University of Petroleum (Beijing) is where those two halves meet. Laboratory TOC measurements are sparse and expensive; conventional logs are continuous. Machine learning is the bridge between them — but only if the geology, the data quality and the validation scheme are handled honestly first.

I use computation as a way of reasoning about rocks, not as a replacement for it. A model that predicts well but cannot be explained petrophysically is not a finished piece of work.

PROFESSIONAL JOURNEY

UPPER TRIASSIC XUJIAHE FORMATION · SICHUAN BASIN · CHINA

Machine-learning prediction of total organic carbon from conventional well logs

Master's thesis research, September 2024 – present. Supervised by Prof. Song Zezhang, College of Geosciences, China University of Petroleum (Beijing).

The problem. Total organic carbon controls whether a source rock is worth anything, and it is measured in the laboratory on a handful of core plugs per well. Conventional logs — gamma ray, resistivity, density, neutron, sonic — are recorded continuously over hundreds of metres. The research question is how far, and under what conditions, a continuous TOC profile can be reconstructed from log response in a heterogeneous tight clastic system, and where that reconstruction stops being trustworthy.
